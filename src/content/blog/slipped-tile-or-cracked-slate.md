"Slipped tile" and "cracked slate" get used as catch-all terms for any roof damage involving tiles or slates. They're actually two quite different problems with different causes, different urgency, and different fixes.

## What's a slipped tile?

A slipped tile is one that's moved out of position. Usually you can see it — there's a gap in the roofline, a tile lower than its neighbours, or a tile sitting at the bottom of the roof slope where it shouldn't be.

The most common causes:

- **Failure of the nail or fixing** — every tile is held by at least one nail or clip. Old nails (especially on Victorian terraces) corrode and eventually let go. The tile doesn't fall off — it slides down the roof until something stops it.
- **Movement of the batten** — the wooden batten the tile hooks onto can warp or rot. The tile can still be intact, but its support has gone.
- **Wind damage** — high winds can lift tiles that were marginally fixed. Sometimes the tile comes off entirely, sometimes it just shifts.
- **Foot traffic** — anyone walking on a tile roof who shouldn't be (including over-confident roofers and over-curious homeowners) can dislodge tiles.

A slipped tile is rarely urgent in the moment, but it can let water in through the gap it leaves. The felt underneath usually catches the water for a while, but it's not designed as a long-term waterproof layer.

## What's a cracked slate?

A cracked slate is exactly what it sounds like: a slate that's split. Natural slate is brittle. It can crack from:

- **Impact** — a branch falling, a slipped tile from higher up hitting it, foot traffic on the roof.
- **Frost damage** — water gets into small pores in the slate, freezes, expands, and the slate cracks. Common after a hard winter on older slate roofs.
- **Age** — old Welsh slate, particularly from the Victorian quarries, gets brittle with age. We've replaced 80-year-old slates that have simply crumbled when touched.
- **"Delamination"** — some imported slates (particularly cheap Chinese slate from the 1990s and 2000s) delaminate in layers rather than crack cleanly. They look fine on top and fall apart underneath. A known industry problem; if you've got a 15-25 year old roof and the slates are mysteriously failing, this is often why.

A cracked slate usually stays in place for a while because of the overlap from the slates above and below. But the crack lets water through, and over time the crack gets worse.

## How to tell from the ground

- **Tile displaced, still on the roof** — slipped tile.
- **Tile missing from the roof, found on the ground or in the gutter** — slipped or blown-off tile.
- **Tile visible but cracked** — usually visible as a darker line across the tile.
- **Slate with a hairline crack, often diagonal** — cracked slate.
- **Bits of slate in the gutter** — flakey, layered debris means delamination.

## How urgent is each?

**Slipped tile, gap visible**: not urgent in dry weather, but worth fixing within a few weeks before the next rain. A single missing tile can let in a lot of water in a storm.

**Cracked slate, no displacement**: less urgent than a slipped tile, but the crack will grow. Worth fixing within a few months.

**Multiple slipped tiles or widespread cracking**: more urgent. The felt underneath is doing the work it wasn't designed for. The longer this goes on, the more expensive the eventual repair.

**Slates crumbling when touched or delaminating in layers**: the slate itself has failed. If it's a known batch problem, the whole roof may need re-slating.

## What the fix usually looks like

- **Slipped tile**: lift the surrounding tiles, hook the displaced one back onto a new fixing (or replace the nail), put it back in place. Quick job.
- **Cracked slate**: usually replaced. Lifting the slates above and "nibbling" out the broken one with a slate ripper, sliding a new slate in, fixing it with a copper nail. Standard technique.
- **Multiple failures**: we usually recommend a partial strip and re-slate rather than individual replacements — quicker, cheaper per slate, and gives us a chance to check the battens and felt underneath.
